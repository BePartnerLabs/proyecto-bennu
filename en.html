Concept and philosophy

Why "Bennu Project"? The name invokes the sacred bird of ancient Egypt that precedes the myth of the Phoenix — the symbol of rebirth on the first mound of earth to emerge after a flood.

«In ancient Egypt, the Bennu was the sacred bird linked to the flooding of the Nile, the Sun, and creation. It was the symbol of resurrection and the continuous cycle of life.» Origin of the project's name
Project mission To transform a seismic tragedy and its resulting debris into new solid ground — safe, ecological, and a driver of urban, tourism, and infrastructural development for the country.
The origin of the Phoenix The Bennu is the direct historical ancestor of the Phoenix. When the Greeks learned the legend of this great golden bird that was reborn, they adapted it into their own culture and called it Phoenix.
The metaphor of rebirth The Bennu was intimately linked to water and land. After floods, it would appear perched on the first mound of emerged earth, announcing a new era of abundance and construction.

The physical challenge: Central Coast bathymetry

The central coast has a complex geological particularity: it has almost no continental shelf. A few hundred meters from shore, the seabed drops toward the Bonaire Trench and the slopes associated with the San Sebastián seismogenic fault system — the boundary between the Caribbean and South American plates.

Rejected

Eastern sector · Tanaguarena

Bathymetric profile: critical slope, near-immediate drop (> -1,000 m). Risk: the weight of the debris on such a steep underwater slope could trigger gravitational landslides (turbidites) and a local tsunami from mass displacement.

Structural collapse and local tsunami risk

Approved

Western sector · Catia La Mar

Bathymetric profile: a shallow, favorable shelf, between 4 and 15 m deep in the first 100–300 m. Advantage: a stable geological base to support the dike without landslide risk, sheltered from eastern currents by the Cabo Blanco headland, with ample road access for machinery flow.

Geologically viable and safe

Technical viability exists by concentrating most of the volume toward Catia La Mar. Should a second polder be needed, it could be sited in the same Cabo Blanco area or toward Tanaguarena, using structures with less coastal penetration to avoid slope collapse. Precisely determining the site requires verifying the bathymetric studies that already exist for the area.

04 / 12

Matrix of alternatives

Conventional disposal alternatives would trigger either a logistical collapse or an ecocide of the Caribbean Sea. Three routes evaluated for 7 M m³ of debris, only one viable.

Rejected

A · Haul the debris to Caracas

Logistics: more than 280,000 dump-truck trips (25 m³/truck). Consequences: collapse of the Caracas–La Guaira highway, excessive fuel consumption, severe road wear, and paralysis of the capital's supply chain.

Technically and logistically unfeasible

Rejected

B · Free dumping into the Caribbean Sea

Ecological impact: heavy metals, asbestos, hydrocarbons, and unclassified organic matter. Consequences: a sediment plume that suffocates reefs and marine life, chemical leaching, and irreparable damage to fishing and tourism.

Unacceptable ecocide

Selected

C · Bennu Project

Isolation and land reclamation. Hermetic containment in a protected marine pit via a perimeter dike (polder), which remediates the coastline and creates 40 hectares of new usable land.

A modern, sustainable, and advantageous solution

The technical solution

Instead of transporting the debris, modern civil engineering allows hermetically confined cells to be built: constructing a dike, draining the water, and filling the dry pit with previously selected debris — the empoldering technology proven in the Netherlands, Japan, and Singapore.

1

Outer shield dike

Giant basaltic rocks (riprap) or concrete caissons that break the wave's kinetic energy before the first cubic meter of debris is placed.

2

Geotextile membrane

A high-strength, marine-grade internal filter that blocks 100% of toxic dust and silt from reaching the sea, allowing only water to pass through. The pit is drained with high-flow pumps.

3

Dry pit and fill

The space reclaimed from the sea is filled strictly dry with crushed inert aggregate — clean concrete, bricks, tiles, and stone — with severe dynamic compaction in layers to prevent soil liquefaction in future earthquakes.

4

Land-side green filter

Upstream triage stations in the affected areas: plastic, wood, metals, and asbestos are separated before disposal. Only already-classified inert aggregate reaches the pit.

+

Tsunami control

Deep-rooted halophyte species — coastal mangroves and sea grapes — planted along the spit's edges dampen wave energy and minor storm surges or tsunamis.

Site and polder design

The coastal polder is planned for one of the coastline's gentlest underwater slopes: the seafront of the Cabo Blanco sector (Catia La Mar parish), between the Mamo and La Zorra streams. The natural Cabo Blanco headland shields coastal sedimentation from eastern currents, minimizing longshore drift and scouring.
